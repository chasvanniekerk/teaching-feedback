Learning preferences

How do you prefer to learn?

Learning preferences describe how we tend to prefer information to be presented and experienced. Start with the VARK model, then look beyond preference to an equally important question: what do we actually do with information once we receive it?

1 · The starting point

Introducing VARK

Neil D. Fleming, a New Zealand educator, developed VARK after becoming interested in why the same teaching did not produce the same learning for every student. He introduced the model in 1987. In 1992, Neil D. Fleming and Colleen Mills published the foundational paper Not Another Inventory, Rather a Catalyst for Reflection.

1992Fleming & Colleen Mills publish the foundational paper.

VARK describes four broad preferences for working with information. Explore the four areas below.

A preference for information communicated through sound and speech. This can include listening to explanations, asking questions, talking ideas through and participating in discussion.

A preference for information represented as words. This includes reading explanations, articles and lists as well as making notes, writing summaries and expressing ideas in written form.

A preference for learning through experience and connection with real situations: examples, demonstrations, practice, simulation, experimentation and applying ideas.

A preference, not a label

VARK describes preferences rather than abilities or fixed types of learner. A preference does not mean that someone can only learn in one particular way.

2 · Look a little closer

Receiving information and producing information

The Neanderthal and the spark

Imagine a Neanderthal banging two rocks together. The movement is kinesthetic, but at this point he is simply acting. He has not yet learned anything about producing a spark.

The rocks strike and a spark appears. He sees the spark. Through this visual information, the consequence of his action becomes available to him: striking the rocks can produce a spark.

He now has something to act upon. When he deliberately strikes the rocks together again to reproduce the spark, the kinesthetic action is no longer merely movement. It is productive learning built on information that was first received and recognised.

“If I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of Giants.” — Isaac Newton

Language-skills pedagogy makes a useful distinction between receptive and productive skills, and the Neanderthal story helps illustrate the relationship between them. We generally need to see, read or listen before we can meaningfully act on what we have learned through writing, discussion or practice.

This concept forms the backbone of the developing DidacticsLab model. It builds on VARK by distinguishing between passive and active learning preferences, and by exploring how receptive learning provides the foundation for productive learning.

What does this reveal?

Aural contains both listening and speaking/discussion. Read/Write contains both reading and writing. But the distinction may be more important than simply separating the activities. Productive learning needs something to act upon: information must first become available to the learner before it can be deliberately discussed, written about or applied. Receptive learning therefore provides the informational foundation on which productive learning can build. Preference is only part of the story. Receptive learning provides information and knowledge to work with. Learning can then be strengthened when learners actively engage with what they have received — for example by discussing it, writing about it or applying it in practice. The relationship between receptive and active learning forms the basis of Six Preferences for Active and Receptive Knowledge Strategies (SPARKS).

3 · Beyond preference

The SPARKS model

SPARKS — Six Preferences for Active and Receptive Knowledge Strategies — starts from the idea that preference is only part of the story. Knowing how information is presented does not tell us how deeply a learner engages with it. Reading can be passive, or it can involve questioning, connecting and generating explanations. Discussion can be superficial, or it can require learners to explain, challenge and build understanding together.

QUESTION 1

How do I prefer information?

This is the territory of learning preference: visual, aural, read/write and kinesthetic forms of information.

QUESTION 2

What do I do with it?

This is cognitive engagement: receiving information is only the beginning. Learners can act on it, construct with it, explain it, apply it and interact with others around it.

↓

From learning preference to learning effectively

This is where DidacticsLab will continue exploring the relationship between the information we prefer and the strategies that help us learn. The goal is not to put learners into boxes, but to help them understand their preferences and become more deliberate about what they do with information.

4 · Building on knowledge

Standing on the shoulders of giants

We do not usually have to discover everything from scratch. Information can be received from teachers, books, demonstrations, observations and other people and developed into knowledge. That prior knowledge gives us something to question, discuss, write about, test and apply.

We need something to act on

Newton's famous “shoulders of giants” metaphor captures a simple idea: existing knowledge can become the foundation from which further knowledge is developed. Productive learning does not emerge from nothing. We first need information, experience or prior knowledge that we can act upon.

SPARKS also builds on what came before

SPARKS does not seek to replace VARK. The work of Neil D. Fleming and Colleen Mills provides an important foundation for thinking about preferences for working with information. VARK offers a simple and accessible language for reflecting on those preferences. SPARKS builds on that foundation by exploring an additional question: how do receptive and active learning strategies relate to one another?

In the same way that learners build on knowledge they have already received, new ideas in education build on the work of those who came before them.

Learn from what came before. Act on what you know. Learn from what happens next.

5 · The six strategies

Introducing VLR–DWK

Within the SPARKS framework, VLR–DWK separates six intentional learning strategies into two related groups: how we receive information and how we act upon what we have learned. It builds on ideas within VARK while making the relationship between receptive and active strategies explicit.

SPARKS
Six Preferences for Active and Receptive Knowledge Strategies

The VLR–DWK model

VLR–DWK is the model within the SPARKS framework. VLR represents the receptive strategies Visual, Listening and Reading. DWK represents the active strategies Discussing, Writing and Kinesthetic. Between them sits knowledge and understanding: what has been received is processed before it can be meaningfully expressed, questioned, tested or applied.

What changed from VARK?

Aural is separated into Listening and Discussing. Read/Write is separated into Reading and Writing. Visual remains on the receptive side and Kinesthetic remains on the active side. The double-barrelled name deliberately makes that distinction visible.

Active does not simply mean moving

An activity is not necessarily active learning. In VLR–DWK, the active side means acting on prior knowledge. The result of that action can then provide new information to see, hear or read — feeding the receptive side again.

6 · Learning continues

The SPARKS learning cycle

Active learning does not end the process. Discussing, writing and practising can produce new information and feedback. That information can be received again, processed and used to refine what the learner does next.

Back to the spark

When the Neanderthal deliberately strikes the rocks again, the result creates new information. He observes what happens, develops his understanding and can adjust what he does next. Productive action therefore creates another opportunity for receptive learning.

Receive. Understand. Act. Learn from what happens next.
