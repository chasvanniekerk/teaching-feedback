Model in development

SPARKS is a model in development. Its concepts, questionnaire and results are being explored and refined. The model has not yet been scientifically validated, and results should be interpreted as indications of learning preferences, not as diagnoses or fixed learning styles.

Learning preferences

Introduction

What are learning preferences?

A learning preference describes the way a person generally prefers to receive, process or work with information when learning.

Awareness

Being aware of your learning preferences can help you make more deliberate choices about how you approach different learning activities.

Limitations

Learning preferences are not fixed categories that define a person; they reflect general tendencies that may change depending on the learning situation, the task and other influencing factors.

From preferences to SPARKS

SPARKS was developed by reviewing and combining insights from several educational models, including VARK, ICAP, and different learning situations. What began as an exploration of learning preferences evolved into a model for designing effective learning strategies.

1 · Desk research · learning preference

Introducing VARK

What is VARK?

VARK is a learning-preference model developed by Neil Fleming to help learners reflect on how they prefer to receive and work with information.

Imagine that you need to learn how to use an unfamiliar coffee machine. Would you look for a diagram of its controls, ask someone to explain it, read the instructions, or work through a demonstration and try making a coffee? These are different ways of approaching the same learning task. VARK gives us a shared vocabulary for talking about these choices.

The letters stand for Visual, Aural, Read / Write and Kinesthetic. A preference is an approach you tend to choose when learning or communicating information. It is different from an ability: enjoying an explanation in one format does not, by itself, show how well you have understood it.

VARK considers both taking in information and working with it. For example, Aural includes listening and speaking, while Read / Write includes reading and expressing ideas in writing. The four descriptions below explain each preference. The coffee-machine examples are illustrations of the categories, not a test of your learning ability.

Why was VARK developed?

Fleming and Mills wanted to help students think about how they were learning and make deliberate changes to their study methods. Their 1992 paper presented a questionnaire as a starting point for that reflection. Completing it was intended to lead to a conversation about what a learner could do differently when studying.

This addresses a practical difficulty: a student may spend considerable time studying without examining the approach they are using. If they struggle, “study harder” gives them little guidance about what to change. VARK offers a way to explore alternatives: could they organise ideas in a diagram, talk them through, put an explanation into words, or connect them to a practical example?

The purpose is to turn awareness into action. Recognising a preference becomes useful when the student uses it to consider a study strategy, tries that strategy and checks whether they can now explain or apply the material. A questionnaire result alone does not establish that learning has improved.

A Brief History

Neil D. Fleming, a New Zealand educator, developed VARK after becoming interested in why the same teaching did not produce the same learning for every student. He introduced the model in 1987. In 1992, Neil D. Fleming and Colleen Mills published the foundational paper Not Another Inventory, Rather a Catalyst for Reflection.

The VARK Model

VARK
Four learning preferences
V

Visual

Diagrams · charts · patterns

Visual refers to representing relationships through diagrams, maps, graphs and symbols. The organisation of information matters: a flowchart can show where a decision leads, while a graph can make a trend visible.

The coffee-machine example: you look for a labelled diagram and a flowchart connecting the controls to the steps for making a coffee.

In VARK, Visual does not mean everything you can see. Reading a written instruction belongs to Read / Write; watching a realistic demonstration can involve Kinesthetic learning.

A

Aural

Listening · speaking · discussion

Aural means information communicated through speech and sound. It includes listening to an explanation, asking questions and explaining an idea aloud. Discussion belongs here too: the learner may be speaking as well as listening.

The coffee-machine example: you ask someone to talk you through the controls, then describe the steps back to them to clarify the sequence.

The defining feature is the use of spoken language. A recorded explanation can involve this preference even when nobody else is present.

R

Read / Write

Reading · writing · text

Read / Write concerns information expressed in written words. This includes instructions, descriptions and lists, as well as producing written explanations. Reading and writing are grouped together within VARK.

The coffee-machine example: you read the instruction booklet, then write a short checklist of the steps you need to follow.

Written activity can serve different purposes. Copying an instruction records it; explaining in your own words why a step is needed requires further thought. The format alone does not tell us how deeply the learner is engaging.

K

Kinesthetic

Experience · practice · application

Kinesthetic connects learning with concrete experiences. It includes realistic examples, demonstrations, simulations and trying things in practice. It is broader than physical movement: a case can connect an abstract idea to a real situation.

The coffee-machine example: you watch someone make a coffee, then use the machine yourself and notice what happens when you change a setting.

The connection between the explanation and an actual experience is central. Moving around while reading does not automatically make the content Kinesthetic in VARK’s sense.

Can you prefer more than one approach?

Yes. VARK calls a preference for two or more approaches multimodal. You might read the coffee-machine instructions and then try the machine, or combine a diagram with a spoken explanation. You do not have to choose one category and use it for every task.

Why does this matter for a teacher?

When a student says “I don’t understand”, you need to find out what they have already tried. VARK provides concrete questions for that conversation: have they read an explanation, examined a diagram, talked through the idea or worked with an example? This helps you move from general encouragement to a specific next step the student can try.

For example, a student keeps rereading a paragraph about supply and demand but cannot explain it. You could ask them to draw the relationship, explain it aloud or work through what happens to a familiar product when demand rises. These are possible alternatives to investigate. Afterwards, ask the student to explain the relationship in a new example so that you can check their understanding.

The teaching value is in helping students make deliberate choices and evaluate those choices. A preference should not restrict the approaches available to them, and matching instruction to a reported preference does not guarantee better learning.

2 · Desk research · cognitive engagement

Introducing ICAP

A second established perspective helps us look beyond preference. In 2014, Michelene T. H. Chi and Ruth Wylie introduced the ICAP framework to describe different modes of cognitive engagement. Rather than asking how information is presented, ICAP looks at what learners overtly do while engaging with learning material.

ICAP
Interactive · Constructive · Active · Passive
P

Passive

The learner receives information without another overt learning action, for example attending to an explanation or reading.

A

Active

The learner physically manipulates or acts on the learning material, for example highlighting or repeating information.

C

Constructive

The learner generates an externalised output that goes beyond the information provided, such as an explanation or inference.

I

Interactive

Learners constructively engage with one another, building on each other's contributions through meaningful dialogue.

Passive → Active → Constructive → Interactive

Preference and engagement are different questions

VARK helps us think about preferences for working with information. ICAP helps us think about cognitive engagement with learning material. A preferred way of receiving information does not by itself tell us how deeply a learner will engage with it. Chi and Wylie's ICAP hypothesis predicts greater learning as engagement moves from Passive through Active and Constructive to Interactive, while recognising important conditions and caveats.

Why the terminology matters

ICAP uses Passive and Active as specific categories of cognitive engagement. SPARKS does not need to reproduce these categories exactly. ICAP is one of the foundations we build upon: it demonstrates that receiving information and engaging with it are meaningfully different, while also distinguishing Active, Constructive and Interactive forms of engagement.

Bringing VARK and ICAP Together

VARK explores how learners prefer to receive and work with information, while ICAP examines how they engage cognitively with it. Together, these perspectives informed the development of VLR–DWK, which distinguishes receiving information from actively working with it.

Learning situations are introduced later as an additional consideration when choosing appropriate learning strategies within SPARKS.

3 · Our synthesis · six strategies

Introducing VLR–DWK

Within the SPARKS framework, VLR–DWK separates six intentional learning strategies into two related groups: how we receive information and how we actively/productively work with what we have learned. It builds on ideas within VARK and ICAP without attempting to reproduce either model.

Why six strategies?

VARK describes four preference categories, while ICAP describes four modes of engagement. These are eight terms across two different frameworks, not eight interchangeable learning strategies. In developing VLR–DWK, we separate VARK’s Aural into Listening and Discussing, and Read/Write into Reading and Writing. Visual remains receptive, while Kinesthetic is placed on the productive side of our model.

Discussing and Writing can support the constructive generation of explanations and ideas; meaningful dialogue can also support ICAP’s Interactive mode. However, simply talking or writing does not automatically make learning Constructive or Interactive. The cognitive engagement depends on what the learner actually does.

The VLR–DWK model

VLR–DWK is the model within the SPARKS framework. VLR represents the receptive strategies Visual, Listening and Reading. DWK represents the active/productive strategies Discussing, Writing and Kinesthetic. Between them sits knowledge and understanding: what has been received is processed before it can be meaningfully expressed, questioned, tested or applied.

What changed from VARK?

Aural is separated into Listening and Discussing. Read/Write is separated into Reading and Writing. Visual remains on the receptive side and Kinesthetic remains on the active side. The double-barrelled name deliberately makes that distinction visible.

Active does not simply mean moving

In SPARKS, Active / Productive is our umbrella for deliberately working with prior information or knowledge. It is not intended to map directly onto ICAP’s specific Active category: a DWK strategy may involve activity that ICAP would classify as Active, Constructive or Interactive. The result can then provide new information to see, hear or read — feeding the receptive side again.

4 · From desk research to analysis

Receiving information and producing information

The Neanderthal and the spark

Imagine a Neanderthal banging two rocks together. The movement is kinesthetic, but at this point he is simply acting. He has not yet learned anything about producing a spark.

The rocks strike and a spark appears. He sees the spark. Through this visual information, the consequence of his action becomes available to him: striking the rocks can produce a spark.

He now has something to act upon. When he deliberately strikes the rocks together again to reproduce the spark, the kinesthetic action is no longer merely movement. It is productive learning built on information that was first received and recognised.

“If I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of Giants.” — Isaac Newton

Looking across the ideas introduced above raises a useful distinction. Language-skills pedagogy separates receptive and productive skills, and the Neanderthal story helps illustrate the relationship between them. We generally need to see, read or listen before we can meaningfully act on what we have learned through writing, discussion or practice.

This concept forms the backbone of the developing DidacticsLab model. This is where our analysis begins. We explore whether the preferences and engagement ideas above can be reorganised into receptive and productive learning strategies, and how those strategies may relate to one another.

Does practice really make perfect?

“Practice makes perfect” is a familiar saying. Learning research supports the underlying principle: learners generally benefit when they do more than simply receive information and instead retrieve, generate, apply or otherwise actively work with what they have learned.

SLAMECKA & GRAF · 1978

Generating rather than only reading

Across five experiments, participants remembered material they generated themselves better than the same material when it was simply presented for reading. Their work established what became known as the generation effect.

ROEDIGER & KARPICKE · 2006

Retrieving rather than only restudying

Students first studied prose passages and then either restudied them or practised retrieving them. Repeated study helped more on an immediate test, but retrieval practice produced substantially better retention after delays of two days and one week.

KARPICKE & BLUNT · 2011

Retrieving and reconstructing knowledge

Retrieval practice produced greater gains in meaningful learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping, including on questions that assessed comprehension and required inference.

FREEMAN ET AL. · 2014

Active learning in the classroom

A meta-analysis of 225 undergraduate STEM studies found higher examination and concept-inventory performance under active learning than under traditional lecturing, alongside lower failure rates.

These studies do not validate SPARKS itself, and they investigate different forms of learning activity under different conditions. Together, however, they provide evidence consistent with a central SPARKS proposition: under normal learning conditions, receiving information and subsequently working actively with that information will generally produce stronger learning than receiving information alone.

What does this reveal?

Aural contains both listening and speaking/discussion. Read/Write contains both reading and writing. But the distinction may be more important than simply separating the activities. Productive learning needs something to act upon: information must first become available to the learner before it can be deliberately discussed, written about or applied. Receptive learning therefore provides the informational foundation on which productive learning can build. Preference is only part of the story. Receptive learning provides information and knowledge to work with. Learning can then be strengthened when learners actively engage with what they have received — for example by discussing it, writing about it or applying it in practice. The relationship between receptive and active learning forms the basis of Six Preferences for Active and Receptive Knowledge Strategies (SPARKS).

5 · Synthesis

The SPARKS model

SPARKS — Six Preferences for Active and Receptive Knowledge Strategies — starts from the idea that preference is only part of the story. VARK gives us a language for thinking about learning preferences, while ICAP gives us a framework for thinking about cognitive engagement. Our analysis adds a third distinction: receptive and productive learning strategies. SPARKS brings these ideas together into a developing framework and asks how passive/receptive and active/productive strategies may relate to one another.

QUESTION 1

How do I prefer information?

This is the territory of learning preference: visual, aural, read/write and kinesthetic forms of information.

QUESTION 2

What do I do with it?

This is the question of cognitive engagement explored by ICAP: learners may receive information, act on it, generate beyond it, or construct understanding interactively with others.

From learning preference to learning effectively

This is where DidacticsLab will continue exploring the relationship between the information we prefer and the strategies that help us learn. The goal is not to put learners into boxes, but to help them understand their preferences and become more deliberate about what they do with information.

6 · Building on knowledge

Standing on the shoulders of giants

We do not usually have to discover everything from scratch. Information can be received from teachers, books, demonstrations, observations and other people and developed into knowledge. That prior knowledge gives us something to question, discuss, write about, test and apply.

We need something to act on

Newton's famous “shoulders of giants” metaphor captures a simple idea: existing knowledge can become the foundation from which further knowledge is developed. Productive learning does not emerge from nothing. We first need information, experience or prior knowledge that we can act upon.

SPARKS also builds on what came before

SPARKS does not seek to replace the work that came before it. The work of Neil D. Fleming and Colleen Mills provides an important foundation for thinking about preferences for working with information. VARK offers a simple and accessible language for reflecting on those preferences.

The work of Michelene T. H. Chi and Ruth Wylie provides another important foundation. ICAP distinguishes modes of cognitive engagement and helps make clear that the way information is encountered is not the same question as what a learner does with it.

SPARKS builds on these foundations without reproducing either model. VARK and ICAP remain established frameworks in their own right; SPARKS is our developing synthesis, exploring an additional relationship: how passive/receptive and active/productive learning strategies may work together. In the same way that learners build on knowledge they have already received, new ideas in education build on the work of those who came before them.

Learn from what came before. Act on what you know. Learn from what happens next.

7 · Learning continues

The SPARKS learning cycle

Active / productive learning does not end the process. Discussing, writing and practising can produce new information and feedback. That information can be received again, processed and used to refine what the learner does next.

Back to the spark

When the Neanderthal deliberately strikes the rocks again, the result creates new information. He observes what happens, develops his understanding and can adjust what he does next. Productive action therefore creates another opportunity for receptive learning.
