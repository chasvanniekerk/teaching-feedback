Model in development

SPARKS is a model in development. Its concepts, questionnaire and results are being explored and refined. The model has not yet been scientifically validated, and results should be interpreted as indications of learning preferences, not as diagnoses or fixed learning styles.

Applying SPARKS

SPARKS becomes practical when learning strategies are considered not only by what learners do, but also by when they do it. This page explores how receptive and productive strategies might be deliberately sequenced in teaching and learning.

1. COGNITIVE OVERLOAD

Cognitive Overload

Imagine an enthusiastic professor delivering an energetic 90-minute lecture. The students are attentive. They follow the examples, respond to the professor and appear fully engaged throughout.

The students were engaged. But what did they learn?

At the end of the lecture, the professor asks students to reconstruct the main ideas. The response is disappointing. They remember fragments, but much of the explanation cannot be recalled or connected.

Attention is necessary for learning, but attention alone does not guarantee that everything presented will be processed, organised and retained. A continuous stream of new information can place substantial demands on the learner's limited cognitive resources.

The Problem SPARKS Wants To Address

The professor may have succeeded in holding attention while still giving learners too little protected opportunity to work with what they were receiving. SPARKS asks what learners are being asked to receive, what they are being asked to produce, and how those demands are organised over time.

This scenario illustrates a possible cognitive-overload problem; it is not a diagnostic test. Poor recall after a lecture can have several causes. The purpose of the example is to expose the difference between visible engagement and demonstrated learning.

2. COGNITIVE LOAD

What Is Cognitive Load?

Cognitive Load Theory is associated particularly with the work of John Sweller and was later developed extensively with researchers including Paul Ayres and Slava Kalyuga. The theory begins from a central instructional problem: working memory has limited capacity when dealing with novel information, while long-term memory can organise knowledge into structures that make later processing more efficient.

I

Intrinsic Cognitive Load

Interacting concepts · complex calculations

Some cognitive demand comes from the material itself and the number of interacting elements that must be understood. What is difficult for a novice may become much easier once relevant knowledge has been organised in long-term memory.

E

Extraneous Cognitive Load

Distracting slides · unclear instructions

Additional demand can be created by the way instruction is designed. Cognitive Load Theory therefore asks educators to avoid consuming limited processing resources unnecessarily when those resources could instead support learning.

Why This Matters For SPARKS

SPARKS does not claim that cognitive load is caused simply by using more than one learning strategy. Instead, it asks whether the particular demands placed on a learner at the same time compete for limited processing resources. That question gives us a research basis for examining the timing of VLR and DWK strategies.

Literature Foundation

John Sweller, Paul Ayres and Slava Kalyuga (2011), Cognitive Load Theory, Springer. This book provides a comprehensive treatment of human cognitive architecture, intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load, and instructional effects including split attention, modality and redundancy. The theoretical foundation traces back to John Sweller (1988), Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning, which examined how processing demands can consume cognitive capacity that would otherwise be available for learning.

3. LEARNING STRATEGY

A Strategy Is A Plan

A strategy does more than identify activities. It organises actions over time. In SPARKS, this means considering which strategy a learner is using, what that strategy is intended to achieve, and when it should occur within the learning sequence.

What → When → Why

What is the learner doing? When should it happen? Why is that strategy being used at that point in the learning process?

The Practical Question

Instead of asking only “Which learning activities should I use?”, SPARKS asks an additional question: “In what sequence should learners receive information and actively work with it?”

4. LEARNING SEQUENCE

Complementary, Not Simultaneous

Receptive and productive strategies can complement one another without needing to happen at the same moment. SPARKS explores the proposition that deliberate sequencing may help learners concentrate their available cognitive resources on the task required at that point in time.

Combination Does Not Require Simultaneity

A lesson can combine several SPARKS strategies across time. The developing proposition is not “one strategy is best”, but that teachers should deliberately consider whether simultaneous demands compete for attention and whether separating them would create a clearer learning sequence.

This sequencing principle is a developing SPARKS proposition. It should not yet be read as a universal rule. Its boundaries need to be tested against research on working memory, cognitive load, dual-task interference, task switching, note-taking and multimedia learning.

5. CLASSROOM EXAMPLE

Listen And Take Notes?

A common classroom instruction asks learners to listen to new information while simultaneously deciding what matters and writing it down. SPARKS makes those demands visible and asks whether they should occur together or be deliberately sequenced.

Simultaneous Demand

The teacher continues explaining while the learner is expected to listen, interpret, select important information and write notes at the same time.

LReceive the ongoing explanation
WProduce notes while new information continues

Deliberate Sequence

The teacher creates separate moments for reception and production, allowing the learner to focus on the purpose of each stage.

LListen to the explanation
↧Process and understand
WRetrieve and reconstruct in notes

The Productive Phase Is Part Of The Learning

The pause for writing is not simply time away from instruction. When learners retrieve and reconstruct what they understood, the productive activity may itself create another opportunity for learning and expose gaps that need clarification.

6. LEARNING SITUATIONS

What Is The Learner Trying To Accomplish?

Learning strategies do not exist independently of purpose. The strategy that is useful when learning something new may differ from the strategy useful when trying to apply existing knowledge. For SPARKS, a Learning Situation is therefore being investigated as a strategy-neutral description of what the learner is trying to accomplish before choosing how to learn.

A

Acquiring

New terms · unfamiliar concepts · new skills

I need to learn something new.

You encounter information, terminology or a skill that is new to you. Before you can work with it, you need to build an initial knowledge base.

For example, you meet a new inventory concept for the first time and need to learn its meaning and key terms.

You might read an introduction, listen to an explanation or study a diagram, then write a summary or discuss what you have learned. The choice depends on the content and your learning goal.

U

Understanding

Meaning · relationships · reasons

I need to make sense of something.

You have encountered the information, but its meaning, relationships or underlying reasons are not yet clear. Recognising a term or remembering a definition is not necessarily enough to explain it.

For example, you know the definition of safety stock but cannot yet explain why greater demand uncertainty may require more of it.

You might compare examples, draw the relationships, discuss why they occur or explain them in your own words. The aim is to make sense of the knowledge rather than merely encounter it again.

A

Applying

Calculations · procedures · practical tasks

I need to use what I know.

You have relevant knowledge and face a task where it can be used. The challenge is to put a concept, procedure or skill into practice and check whether you can use it appropriately.

For example, you know a safety-stock method and need to calculate a value using the data for a particular product.

You might review a worked example, attempt the task, write out your reasoning and check the result against feedback. Knowing a method and being able to use it are different learning demands.

P

Problem Solving

Investigating · diagnosing · testing causes

I need to resolve something where the solution is not immediately known.

You face a difficulty for which the solution or the route to it is not immediately clear. You need to investigate what is happening, identify gaps in your knowledge and decide what to try.

For example, a warehouse keeps running out of an item even though its recorded stock appears sufficient. Several explanations are possible.

You might examine records, observe the process, discuss possible causes and test an explanation. New information may change your next step; the planned strategy can therefore be revised as you learn.

T

Transferring

New contexts · adapting · connecting

I need to use what I learned in a different situation.

You encounter a context that differs from the one in which you originally learned something. You need to judge what still applies, what has changed and how your knowledge should be adapted.

For example, you learned inventory planning for a manufacturer and now need to use those ideas in a hospital, where demand and service requirements differ.

You might compare the two contexts, discuss the limits of the original approach and test an adapted application. Transfer involves recognising useful connections rather than copying a method unchanged.

A Developing Synthesis

Established frameworks classify learning in different ways. Hattie and Donoghue distinguish phases including acquisition, consolidation and transfer; Koedinger, Corbett and Perfetti distinguish memory and fluency, induction and refinement, and understanding and sense-making learning events; while Anderson and Krathwohl, Merrill, Chi and Wylie, and Laurillard classify other aspects of learning. These categories cannot simply be treated as equivalent. The five situations above are the current SPARKS synthesis emerging from comparison of these frameworks, not an established or validated taxonomy.

7. LESSON DESIGN

Designing With SPARKS

SPARKS can give teachers a simple set of questions for examining the flow of a lesson rather than merely counting how many different activities it contains.

What am I asking learners to receive right now?
What am I asking learners to produce right now?
Am I asking them to do both at the same time?
Would separating these demands improve focus?
Where should reception pause so learners can retrieve, explain or apply?
What new information or feedback should feed the next receptive phase?
